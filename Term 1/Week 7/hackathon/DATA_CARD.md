# Data card and ethical reflection — Knotweed Checker

**Data.** 832 photos from Dutch iNaturalist users, not taken by us: 302 knotweed (*Reynoutria*) and 530 other plants: bindweed, *Persicaria* and *Bistorta* (some native, some garden plants), *Fallopia* (black-bindweed, Russian vine), bramble, nettle and ivy. They are mostly close-ups taken by nature enthusiasts in summer and autumn, often of large, mature plants. Licences are CC0 or CC-BY(-NC/-SA); every photo's author, licence and link is in `dataset/sources.csv`. Labels come from the community-confirmed species, not from our search term. iNaturalist's search mixed knotweed and bindweed, which put about 70 photos in the wrong class until we caught it. We also removed 26 photos of a rust fungus on bramble leaves. The "not knotweed" class covers only these few plant groups, not the whole Dutch flora.

**How we built and tested the model.** Photos were scraped with `scrape_inaturalist.py`: only research-grade observations (identity confirmed by the community) from the Netherlands. In Roboflow we made a classification project where the folder name is the label. We split by plant, not by photo: the 302 knotweed photos come from only 185 plants, so all photos of one plant go in the same split (587/172/73 train/valid/test). Otherwise the model could be tested on a plant it already saw. Training photos were doubled with flips, small rotations and brightness changes (`train_roboflow.py`). Model: Roboflow's ViT classifier (`vit-base-patch16-224-in21k`), version `knotweed-nl/1`. Test (`evaluate.py`, same 70% rule as the app): 95% of the 73 test photos right, 24 of 26 knotweed found, 2 of 47 other plants wrongly flagged. Accuracy alone flatters it: always answering "not knotweed" would already score 64%.

**Second test: new places and all seasons.** Because the Dutch test set is small and comes from the same source as training, we collected 168 more photos from Belgium and Germany (`external_test.py`, credits in `external_test/sources.csv`): one photo per plant, none used in training, split into spring, summer and autumn/winter. Our model: 93% right, 77 of 83 knotweed found, 5 of 85 other plants wrongly flagged. By season: spring 93%, summer 93%, autumn/winter 95%. We also ran a general AI model as is, without training it on our photos (CLIP, `clip_baseline.py`), on the same photos. Both found 77 of 83 knotweed photos, but CLIP wrongly flagged 21 of 85 other plants against 5 for ours.

**Limitations and failure modes.** We expected the model to fail on young spring shoots, because the Dutch photos are mostly summer and autumn. In the Dutch test both missed knotweed photos were from April and May. The bigger, all-season test shows the effect is small: it found 27 of 30 spring knotweed photos against 25 of 26 in summer. Bare brown winter stems and knotweed hidden among other plants remain rare in our data. A plant from a group it never saw in training (anything outside the look-alikes above) may get a random answer. We have not measured separately how it does on photos from far away, in bad light or blurry. Confidence scores are not probabilities you can trust: the model can be 98% sure and wrong. It called a bindweed (*Calystegia sepium*) knotweed with 98% confidence. Because iNaturalist users photograph "interesting" plants, the photos are not like the random snapshot a passer-by takes, so real-world accuracy will probably be lower than our test score. The Dutch test set has only 73 photos (26 knotweed), so one mistake more or less moves its score by about 1.4 percentage points; that is why we added the second test. Both tests still use photos from nature enthusiasts, so a test on our own street photos would be the fairest next step.

**What could go wrong if it were used for real decisions.** A false "not knotweed" means a patch is not reported and keeps spreading, which is the expensive mistake. A false "knotweed" could make someone cut or poison a harmless native plant. Cutting real knotweed without the right method spreads it further, so the app must never replace an expert. It should only decide "worth reporting", and a trained person should check every report before anyone removes a plant. Location data in reports could also reveal where people live, so reports should share only the plant's location.

**What we already did to reduce these risks.** The app only says "likely knotweed" when the model picks knotweed with 70% confidence or more; with less it says "maybe" and asks for a closer photo. Every answer carries a warning that it can be wrong and that an expert should check before anything is removed. The app gives no removal instructions, only "don't cut it, report it". We split the data by plant, so our test score is not inflated by the model seeing the same plant twice. We report how many knotweed photos it missed, not just overall accuracy. The app itself saves no photos or locations, but each photo is sent to Roboflow's servers to be classified, so users should not photograph people or house numbers. We tested on a second, independent set of photos from other countries and seasons, and against a general AI model, instead of trusting one score. **Next steps** would be testing on our own street photos and adding winter images to the training data.

**What surprised us.** We expected the model to fail on young spring shoots, but when we tested it on 168 new photos from Belgium and Germany it found 27 of 30 spring plants. The real problem was somewhere else: iNaturalist's search had mixed up knotweed and bindweed, so about 70 of our photos had the wrong label before we caught it. Testing taught us more than guessing.
